Turn project photos into a useful contractor case study

A practical case-study template for builders: document the scope, constraints, process and outcome without making unsupported claims.

By BuildersHaus · September 28, 2026

A gallery can show attractive work while leaving a prospective customer unsure what your business actually did. A useful case study connects the pictures to the original problem, your role, the constraints and the result. It should help someone with a similar project decide whether to start a conversation.

Start with one real, specific job

Choose a project for which you can confirm the details and use the photographs. Record the type of property, general location, your scope and the date or project period. Name participating businesses only when that attribution is accurate and agreed. A subcontractor’s story should describe its own contribution rather than imply responsibility for the entire building.

Ask what the reader can learn from this example. An occupied renovation might explain access and sequencing. A commercial fit-out might show how trades coordinated around an existing ceiling. A repair might illustrate the information needed before a final scope could be agreed.

Give each photograph a job

  1. Context: show the work area and explain the starting problem.
  2. Process: show a relevant stage and explain the choice or constraint visible in it.
  3. Result: show the completed scope and identify what the photograph demonstrates.

Use captions that add information instead of repeating “before” or “after.” A caption can identify the part of the project, the stage and the business responsible. Check image rights, client permission and the visibility of private information before publishing. If a drawing is conceptual or an image is supplied by a manufacturer, label it accordingly.

A case-study structure you can reuse

Headline: [Work completed] for [property type or project context].

Starting point: What did the customer need to change?

Our scope: What work did your business undertake, and what was outside it?

Constraints: What affected access, sequencing or coordination?

Approach: What decisions were made, and why?

Result: What can be documented? Separate observations from measured results.

Next step: What should a reader send if they have a similar project?

Support claims with the records you actually have

Do not turn a visual improvement into a claim about energy savings, fire performance, structural capacity or customer satisfaction without the appropriate evidence. Explain what was done and identify the limits of the example. Publish a customer quotation only with permission and accurate attribution. Avoid adding an unverified rating to structured data or the page.

Keep a private project record with approved photographs, caption details and the source of any figures used. If the customer changes what may be shared, that record makes it easier to update the story consistently.

Connect the story to a useful next step

Link the case study from the service page it supports, and link back to that service from the story. Refer readers to another guide only when it answers a question raised by the project. A homeowner preparing a similar enquiry can use the BuildersHaus renovation brief; a business planning its content can use our contractor-marketing guide.

Industry Army Marketing is the network’s route for discussing website and content support. Make that relationship clear and keep customer project enquiries directed to the appropriate contractor or project-enquiry route.

Review what the article helps people do

Track whether the page is discoverable, whether readers follow the relevant service link and whether resulting enquiries describe suitable work. Those are different measures. A published page or accepted sitemap is not evidence of a new client.

Google’s people-first content guidance encourages pages that serve an intended audience, while its link guidance explains crawlable links and descriptive anchor text. Use those principles to make the story useful and connected; they do not guarantee a ranking.
